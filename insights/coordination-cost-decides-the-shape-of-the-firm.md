In 1937 Ronald Coase asked "Why do firms exist?" and answered with transaction costs: "It's often cheaper to organize economic activity inside a company than to coordinate it through markets." The other side of the ledger, as Amjad Masad puts it, is that "companies have their own coordination costs: management, meetings, departments, reporting, and bureaucracy," which "for more than a century, we've accepted... as the price of doing business." The implication is that the firm's boundary sits wherever those two costs balance.

Masad's argument is that agents now take on "more of the execution and coordination," and "as AI changes those economics, we have an opportunity to rethink the organization itself: how much a small team can accomplish, how far an individual's expertise can reach, and what a company can attempt." His "Self-Driving Company" divides the work: people define goals, exercise judgment and taste, and stay accountable; agents execute "with clear boundaries for when to act and when to seek approval." His dated bet, made while launching a product for exactly this, is that every Fortune 500 company will be self-driving by the end of 2030.

As a thinking tool, Coase gives a question to ask of any piece of work: is it cheaper to coordinate inside, or to buy it? This suggests AI changes both sides of that question at once. It lowers the internal coordination cost that [[ai-is-steel-context-bearing-systems-lift-the-org-scaling-limit]] describes, which lets a small team hold more. It also lowers the cost of handing work to an outside provider, which is why [[outsourcing-is-the-autopilot-wedge]]. Where the boundary settles is an empirical question for each kind of work. The internal half shows up as [[orchestrator-agent-replaces-human-coordination]] and as [[ic-and-dri-are-the-only-roles-left-without-middle-management]].

Tan walks through a real skill file to show how unmagical it is. When a meeting recording lands, transcribe it with speaker labels, pull out the commitments made and who made them and the deadline, cross-check every person named against the library and link their pages, file the summary here and the full transcript there — and if anything contradicts something already believed, flag it rather than overwrite it. That is the whole artifact: a page of English. The test he draws from it is the portable part: "If a smart intern could follow it, an agent can run it."

The claim underneath is one he notes he has taken flak for and believes more than ever — markdown is actually code, and if you can write clear instructions in English you are a programmer, because the compiler is a language model. The evidence he offers is who is doing it: media people, event staff and finance team members at YC who never open a terminal are writing skill files and scheduling jobs, including a finance colleague who compiled roughly a hundred Excel workbooks into a single app built with an internal agent. Tan's description of her is the point — "She is not a programmer. She is a manager of agents now." This extends [[skills-as-markdown-replace-fine-tuning]] from a cost argument to an access argument, and it is bounded by [[know-which-computation-belongs-in-latent-vs-deterministic-space]]: the intern test tells you the instructions are clear, not that the work belongs in prose rather than in code. The quality bar it implies is the same one behind [[if-it-left-no-artifact-it-didnt-happen]] — writing the procedure down is what makes it executable at all.

Ivan Zhao's sharpest historical lesson is about the steam engine. Early textile factories ran on waterwheels; when the steam engine arrived, "factory owners initially swapped waterwheels for steam engines and kept everything else the same. Productivity gains were modest." The breakthrough came only when they "decouple[d] from water entirely" and "redesigned their factories around steam engines" — then "productivity exploded." His diagnosis of the present: "We're still in the 'swap out the waterwheel' phase. AI chatbots bolted onto existing tools."

The actionable test is whether you're redesigning the work or decorating it: a chatbot pointed at an unchanged workflow is a waterwheel swap.

Garry Tan adds the asymmetry of who can actually perform the redesign. Describing the bureaucratic friction he lived at Microsoft — he and his PM mentor physically walking a bug across a highway to another team that "wouldn't reply to our emails, wouldn't fix our bugs, wouldn't even mark it as won't fix" — he imagines the same coordination handled today by "a markdown file and a skill that is run on a cron," surfacing who is blocked and by whom, so that "you could release something in a day or two that would have taken like six months." Then the asymmetry: "probably an org like Microsoft can't. But a startup can. And every startup must." The redesign is not merely available to small companies; on Tan's account it is obligatory for them and something an org like Microsoft "probably can't" do, which is the same inertia that makes [[institutional-slowness-is-the-runway-not-the-threat]] a window rather than a warning. Extended past the company's own walls, the redesign reaches the interfaces between firms — see [[ai-erases-the-api-line]]. This is the organizational twin of [[scaffolding-is-tech-debt-against-the-next-model]] — both warn against preserving the old structure when the new material's whole value is that it dissolves the old constraint. It is what [[ai-compresses-execution-not-judgment]] looks like operationally (when execution is free, the leverage is in re-imagining the process), and it is the same proactive move as [[self-disruption-follows-the-value-chain-downward]] — redesign before the old shape is competed away. Mistaking the transition form for the final form is exactly [[new-tech-first-imitates-the-medium-it-replaces]].

Tom Blomfield reaches the same diagnosis from YC's vantage point and supplies the alternative unit. Companies are "bolting AI on onto the side of the company" — question-answer bots, lightweight agents that can call a tool like web search — and the ceiling of that approach is making each person twenty or thirty percent more productive. His reframe is to ask what happens if the company is reimagined as a series of self-improving AI loops from the ground up, which he says produces "a very very different conclusion." That is the waterwheel-to-steam redesign made concrete at the level of a component you can actually build: see [[a-learning-mechanism-is-what-separates-a-loop-from-a-pipeline]] for the anatomy, and [[a-human-in-the-loop-caps-the-loop-at-waking-hours]] for why a bolted-on agent stays capped at human speed no matter how good the model gets. Blomfield's own caveat is worth preserving: he opens by saying nobody knows how to do this yet, and that anyone claiming to have it figured out is probably lying.
